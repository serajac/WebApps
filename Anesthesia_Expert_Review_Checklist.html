Workflow, from engagement to report

Work through the phases in order. The order is deliberate: you list the records and fix the governing authorities before judging care, and you build the timeline from objective data before reading narratives or depositions, so the chart and the testimony don't anchor your view of what happened.

  1. Intake and scope. Confirm the engagement, the retaining side, any conflicts, the deadlines and the questions counsel wants answered. Note which expert rules apply: FRE 702 and FRCP 26(a)(2)(B) in federal court, or the state's equivalents. Bates-number and index every record you receive. Goes in: §0 Case identification
  2. Set up the case. Enter the case details. The date of event is the most important field: it sets which version of each authority applies and makes the regulation links open the text as it read on that day. Choose the case type and facility setting, then read the case profile's list of risks. Goes in: §0, case type & setting
  3. Inventory the records. Work through §17 before reviewing care. Score each record type with the scale in the scoring guide (the §17 column explains what each score means for records), and send counsel the list of 2- and 3-point gaps as a records request. Goes in: §17 Missing / additional records
  4. Fix the governing authorities. Classify the facility and identify which laws, board rules, CMS conditions, accreditation standards, professional guidelines and facility policies apply, in the versions in effect on the event date. Press Log beside any authority to add it to the verification log, then record the exact version and pinpoint section. Goes in: §1, §19 Authority log, §20 Source library
  5. Reconstruct the timeline. Build the minute-by-minute chronology from objective timestamps first: monitor or device exports, medication administration times, EMS and 911 records. Add narrative notes afterward and flag any conflicts between sources. Then fill in the critical intervals. Goes in: §15 Reconstruction, §22 Critical intervals
  6. Review care in the core sections. Work through §2–14 in the order care happened: patient selection, preanesthesia evaluation, personnel, equipment, conduct of anesthesia, sedation depth, rescue, recovery, infection control, systems, then the setting module. Score every item and fill in both note fields for anything scored 1 or higher. Goes in: §2–14, plus §CT case-type focus
  7. Analyze the event. Open the §21 module or modules that match what happened (hypoxemia, difficult airway, LAST, arrest and so on). Use the timeline to find the earliest objective sign, when the team recognized it, the first intervention, escalation and transfer. Goes in: §21A–K
  8. Analyze causation. Separate the complication itself from how the team responded to it. State each alleged departure as a specific act or omission, test other possible causes, and say plainly where the data can't answer the question. Goes in: §23 Causation
  9. Integrate testimony. As depositions arrive, log admissions and contradictions with page:line cites, link them to Concern # IDs, and re-score items the testimony confirms or undercuts. Goes in: Deposition notes, Cross-reference, Citation builder
  10. Synthesize. Move every 2- and 3-point finding into the finding-to-authority matrix. Complete the expert analysis summary, keeping opinions the record already supports separate from those that should stay provisional. Goes in: §16 Matrix, §18 Summary
  11. Quality-check and export. Run the pre-report check, then use Copy findings summary for your report draft and Save case file to keep a portable copy. Goes in: toolbar at top
Scoring: what 0, 1, 2, 3 and N/A mean

Each score answers one question: based on the material reviewed so far, how much does this criterion need expert attention? A score is a triage signal for you and counsel. It is not a finding of negligence, and every score stays provisional until the record is complete.

Decision path

  1. Does the criterion apply to this case? No → N/A.
  2. Is there enough information to judge it? No → score at least 1 and add the missing item to §17. Don't use N/A for missing information.
  3. Does the record show it was met? Yes → 0.
  4. Is the problem only in how care was documented, with no apparent effect on care? Yes → 1.
  5. Can you name the authority that may have been departed from? Yes, but relevance is still open → 2.
  6. Is the departure clear, and is it material to the outcome or a core safety failure? Yes → 3.

Scoring discipline

  • Score the care, not the outcome. Ask what was known or knowable at that moment. A bad outcome doesn't raise a score, and a good outcome doesn't lower one.
  • Don't infer undocumented care. An action that "would ordinarily" happen but isn't charted is a documentation question (at least 1) until evidence or testimony confirms it.
  • One fact, several items. The same fact can properly affect more than one criterion (for example, late EMS activation in 8.13 and 21K.5). Score each item on its own terms, and write "same fact as 21K.5" in the analysis so counsel doesn't read it as separate failures.
  • Totals are for triage. One 3-point finding can outweigh twenty 1-point findings. The totals show where concerns cluster; your opinion rests on the analysis.
  • Re-score as the record grows. New records and testimony should move scores both up and down. Note what changed in the analysis field.
Evidence / pinpoint citation: what to write

What it answers: where in the record is the fact that supports this score? Anyone, whether counsel, opposing counsel or you months later, should be able to find the exact page and line in seconds. It records the fact itself, not your opinion about it.

What to include

  • Source document: anesthesia record, MAR, monitor export, PACU flowsheet, EMS run sheet, deposition
  • Exhibit number and Bates number when assigned
  • Page and line(s), or the clock time for records organized by time
  • The fact, briefly quoted or paraphrased (values, times, who did what)
  • Absence: when the point is that something isn't there, list every place you checked
  • Conflicts: cite both sources and state the difference

Examples

StrongAnesthesia Record, Ex. 7, p. 3 (Bates DEF-000142), 10:42–10:47: SpO2 charted 88% → 79%; no airway maneuver charted until 10:49.
Strong (absence)No ETCO2 values on Anesthesia Record Ex. 7 pp. 2–4 or monitor export Ex. 9; Jones Dep. 84:12–25 confirms no capnograph in room.
Strong (conflict)Nursing note Ex. 11 p. 2 records 911 call at 10:55; CAD log Ex. 14 p. 1 time-stamps the call at 11:03 (8-minute discrepancy).
WeakAnesthesia record shows desaturation.

For citation formats (Smith Dep. 84:12–85:4 and so on), see the pinpoint citation section and builder.

Authority / analysis: what to write

This field has two parts. Authority answers what rule or standard applies here? Analysis answers how does the evidence measure up against it, and why does that matter? The evidence field holds the fact; this field holds your reasoning.

Authority: name it precisely

  • Type: statute/regulation · licensing-board rule · CMS condition · accreditation standard · professional standard or guideline · facility policy · manufacturer IFU · peer-reviewed literature
  • Exact document and section (for example, 42 CFR 416.42(a)), not just the organization's name
  • Version in effect on the event date. Never substitute the current version; the page flags links that post-date the event.
  • Applicability: jurisdiction, and whether it applies to this facility (a Medicare-certified ASC, for example)
  • Weight: mandatory (law, regulation, CfC) or evidence of the standard of care (guidelines, policy, literature)

The legal standard of care is set by the jurisdiction's law (for example, Va. Code § 8.01-581.20). Guidelines and policies are evidence of that standard; they don't define it by themselves.

Analysis: four short moves

  1. Required: what the authority called for in this situation
  2. Record: what the evidence shows actually happened (refer to the cite; don't repeat it)
  3. Comparison: met, departed, or can't tell yet, and why
  4. Significance: whether it matters to the event, links to related Concern #s, and what would change your view
Example (illustrative)Authority: ASA Standards for Basic Anesthetic Monitoring, version in effect on [event date], ventilation section. Professional standard, not binding law. Facility sedation policy Ex. 21 § 4.3 adopts it.
Analysis: Continual ventilation monitoring was required during deep sedation. The record shows no capnography (see evidence) and the CRNA confirms it. Likely departure. It matters because the monitor export shows hypoventilation about 4 minutes before desaturation; same fact as 21A.2. Open question: whether earlier capnography would have changed the course (see 23.6).
WeakViolated ASA standards.
Pre-report quality check
  • Every 2- and 3-point item has a pinpoint citation and a named authority, with its version.
  • No current guideline has been substituted for the version in effect on the event date (check the "post-dates event" flags).
  • Mandatory requirements are clearly separated from professional guidance and literature.
  • No undocumented action has been assumed to have occurred.
  • Each 3-point item has a stated link to the injury in §23, or is stated to be a safety concern without a causal link.
  • Opinions sit within your expertise; §18 lists any other experts needed.
  • Records still outstanding are listed in §17, and opinions that depend on them are marked provisional in §18.
  • Concern # IDs match across the checklist, matrix, cross-reference and report.
