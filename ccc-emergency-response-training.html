Start Here · Why you're in this room

You are already an emergency leader. This makes you a prepared one.

🎯 What's in it for you: When something goes wrong on campus, people will not look for the emergency binder — they will look at you. This course gives you the vocabulary, the roles, and the muscle memory so that when the moment comes, your leadership is calm, coordinated, and defensible.

This session is built on a simple idea from adult-learning research (called andragogy): adults learn best when the material respects what they already know, connects to a real problem, and can be used right away. So this is not a lecture. It is a working session. You will make decisions, test your instincts, and leave with things you can act on this week.

🏈 Think of it like a football team the week before the big game. The players already know how to play. Practice isn't about teaching them the sport — it's about running the specific plays together so that on game day nobody has to stop and think about who does what. An emergency is game day. This course is practice.

Module 1 · WHO

Who does what when the alarm sounds

🧭 Why this matters: The single biggest failure in real emergencies is not lack of courage — it's role confusion. Two people doing the same job, or a job nobody picked up. A shared command structure fixes that before the crisis, not during it.

Camp Community College, like every institution that receives federal preparedness support, organizes emergencies using the Incident Command System (ICS) — part of the national standard called NIMS (the National Incident Management System). It sounds bureaucratic. It's actually beautifully simple.

👨‍🍳 ICS is a restaurant kitchen during the dinner rush. One head chef (the Incident Commander) calls the shots. Stations each own their lane — grill, sauté, plating, dish. Nobody wanders. Orders flow one way, food flows the other, and even on the busiest night the kitchen doesn't collapse into chaos — because everyone knows their station. ICS is that kitchen, scaled to a campus emergency.

Tap each role to see who it is and what they own. (These are ICS functions — one person may wear more than one hat on a small campus.)

The one thing to remember

ICS scales. A small event might be one person doing all eight jobs. A campus-wide event fills every box. You don't need a bigger team — you need everyone to know which box they're standing in.

Module 2 · WHAT

What we're preparing for — and what document runs the show

📋 Why this matters: "Emergency" is a wide word. Knowing the categories keeps you from over-reacting to a small event or under-reacting to a growing one — and every one of them is governed by a single plan you should be able to name.

The document that runs everything: the CEMP

Virginia law requires every public college to maintain a Crisis and Emergency Management Plan (CEMP). Think of it as the campus's owner's manual for bad days.

✈️ The CEMP is the seat-back safety card on an airplane. Nobody reads it for fun. But it's written, standardized, and in reach of every seat — so that in the ninety seconds that matter, no one is inventing a plan from scratch. Our job as leaders is to make sure the card exists, is current, and that we've all "found the nearest exit" before we ever need it.

The families of emergencies (tap to expand)

Hurricanes and flooding (very real for Franklin and Suffolk), severe storms, tornadoes, winter weather, extreme heat. These usually give warning time — the leadership challenge is deciding when to close or evacuate.
Active threat or violence, bomb threat, civil disturbance, a missing student, suspicious package. These are sudden — success depends on notification speed and rehearsed lockdown/evacuate decisions.
Power or water loss, hazardous materials spill, fire, gas leak, a cyber incident that knocks out systems (including the notification system itself). The leadership question: what's our backup when the normal tools are down?
Disease outbreak, pandemic, a medical emergency in a crowd. These unfold slowly but broadly, and lean heavily on communication and continuity-of-instruction planning.

Leader's takeaway

You do not need to memorize every hazard. You need to know that one plan — the CEMP — covers all of them, and that your role inside ICS stays the same no matter which family of emergency you're facing. That's the whole point of a standardized system.

Module 3 · WHY

Why we must do this — law, accreditation, and trust

⚖️ Why this matters: Preparedness isn't optional good practice for a Virginia college — it's a legal duty of the governing board and a factor in keeping the college accredited. Leaders who can cite the "why" get resources approved faster.

1. Virginia law — Code § 23.1-804

State law places specific, non-negotiable duties on every public institution of higher education. In plain English:

Key requirements of Virginia Code 23.1-804
The law requires…What that means for leadership
Adopt & keep a current CEMPThe governing board formally owns the plan
Designate & train people to activate emergency alertsNamed individuals — with training on file
Annual review + written certification to VDEMThe President certifies each year to the state
Full review & board re-adoption every 4 yearsA scheduled, documented refresh cycle
Conduct a test or exercise every year + certify itThis is exactly why a tabletop exercise matters

That last line is the hook for this whole project: the annual exercise requirement is met by running a tabletop exercise — the very thing this course is designed to deliver.

2. Accreditation — SACSCOC

Camp Community College's accreditation runs through the Southern Association of Colleges and Schools Commission on Colleges (SACSCOC). Two standards touch emergency readiness directly:

Relevant SACSCOC standards
StandardIn plain terms
13.7 — Physical resourcesThe college maintains and protects its facilities. A CEMP and drills are evidence of that stewardship.
13.8 — Institutional environmentThe college takes reasonable steps to provide a healthy, safe, and secure environment for everyone on campus.
🩺 Accreditation is like a hospital's health inspection. Nobody stays open just to pass inspection — but the inspection exists because a safe, well-run institution is one you can trust with people's futures. Documented emergency preparedness is one of the things the inspector expects to find already in place.

3. The real why: trust

Behind the statute and the standard is the simplest reason of all. Students, families, and employees hand this college their time, money, and safety. Being ready to protect them on the worst day is the clearest possible expression of that trust — and it's what leadership is ultimately for.

Module 4 · WHEN

When to act — and how big to go

⏱️ Why this matters: Most emergencies aren't "on/off." They escalate. Matching your response to the level — no more, no less — is a leadership skill you can practice.
🌡️ Think of a thermostat, not a light switch. You don't run the furnace at full blast to warm a room by two degrees, and you don't ignore a room that's dropping toward freezing. Emergency levels let you dial the response to match the actual heat of the situation.
Level 1
Minor
Localized, handled by on-duty staff. A small water leak, a single medical call, a brief power blip. Leadership is informed, not activated. Documentation still matters.
Level 2
Partial
Affects a building or a group; needs coordination. A gas leak evacuation, a serious storm approaching. A partial command team stands up. Emergency notification likely goes out. Leadership actively leads.
Level 3
Full
Campus-wide, life-safety, or outside help needed. Active threat, major fire, hurricane closure. Full ICS activation, all channels used, outside agencies engaged, continuity plans triggered.

The costly instinct to resist

Leaders often under-declare early — hoping a situation stays small so they don't "overreact." It is almost always cheaper and safer to stand a team up and stand it back down than to scramble late. You can always scale down. Scaling up late costs minutes you can't get back.

The clock that starts immediately

The moment an incident is recognized, three things should happen fast and in parallel — not one after another:

1Protect life
(evacuate / lockdown / shelter)
2Notify
(alert those at risk)
3Coordinate
(stand up ICS, call for help)
Module 5 · HOW

How a response actually unfolds

🔧 Why this matters: When you can picture the sequence, you stop freezing and start leading. This is the reusable pattern behind almost every campus emergency.

Emergency management has four phases. Two happen before anything goes wrong — and that's where leadership has the most power.

The four phases of emergency management
PhaseEveryday analogyLeadership's job
Mitigation — reduce riskInstalling smoke detectorsFund fixes; remove known hazards
Preparedness — get readyRehearsing the fire drillTraining, exercises, this course
Response — actGetting everyone out safelyLead the ICS, make the calls
Recovery — get back to normalCleaning up, rebuilding, checking on peopleContinuity, care, lessons learned
🚪 The whole cycle is a fire drill you never stop running. You check the smoke detectors (mitigation), you rehearse the walk to the exit (preparedness), you walk calmly out when the alarm sounds (response), and you talk afterward about what to do better next time (recovery). Then you do it all again. Readiness is a habit, not an event.

The leader's five moves in the first 15 minutes

  1. Confirm and classify — Is this real? What level? (thermostat, not switch)
  2. Protect life first — Order the protective action: evacuate, lockdown, or shelter-in-place.
  3. Notify — Trigger the emergency alert to those at risk. Clear, short, action-oriented.
  4. Stand up ICS — Name the Incident Commander out loud. Assign the boxes you need.
  5. Connect outward — 911 / local responders and, when warranted, the State via your Liaison.

Say it out loud

The most under-used leadership tool in a crisis is a spoken sentence: "I'm the Incident Commander. Our first objective is life safety. You have Operations, you have Communications." Naming it ends the paralysis.

Module 6 · PRACTICE

Tabletop exercise: "The Tuesday That Wasn't Normal"

🎲 Why this matters: A tabletop exercise (TTX) is a low-stress, discussion-based rehearsal — no evacuating real buildings, just leaders talking through a realistic scenario. It's how you satisfy the annual exercise requirement and, more importantly, how you find the gaps before a real event does.
♟️ A tabletop is chess for your emergency plan. You move the pieces, see what happens, and take the move back if it was a blunder — all without a single real king in danger. The whole point is to make your mistakes here, on paper, where they're free.

The deliverable that makes it "official": the AAR/IP

Every real tabletop ends with an After-Action Report / Improvement Plan (AAR/IP) — a short document listing what worked, what didn't, and who owns each fix by when.
