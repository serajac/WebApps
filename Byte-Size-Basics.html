The machine is a kitchen

Every computer, from a phone to a laptop, does the same four jobs. Once you see them as a kitchen, the specs on the box start making sense.

CPU (processor)The chef

Does the actual work. A faster chef finishes each dish sooner.

RAM (memory)The countertop

Where current work sits. Bigger counter, more dishes at once. Cleared when power goes off.

Storage (SSD/drive)The pantry

Where everything is kept long-term. Survives a power-off.

Operating systemThe manager

Windows, macOS, iOS, Android. Seats customers, assigns the chef, keeps order.

Analogy

Why is my computer slow?

Picture 30 open browser tabs as 30 dishes piled on a small countertop. The chef spends more time shuffling plates than cooking. Close what you aren't using and the counter clears. That's why "more RAM" is usually the first upgrade people suggest.

Hardware vs. software

Hardware
Anything you can physically touch: screen, keyboard, the chips inside.
Software
The instructions that tell hardware what to do. Apps and the operating system are software.
Update
A new version of software. Like a recipe card with the typos fixed and a new lock on the back door.
Driver
A tiny translator so the computer can talk to a printer, mouse or camera.

Files, folders & storage

Your computer is a filing cabinet that never runs out of hanging folders, as long as you name things so you can find them again.

Analogy

The filing cabinet

A file is one document. A folder is a hanging folder. Folders can sit inside folders, like a drawer labeled "Taxes" holding folders for each year.

The little ending on a file name, like .pdf or .jpg, is the label that tells the computer which app opens it. A .jpg is a photo. A .docx is a Word document.

Local vs. cloud

Local storage
Kept on your device. Like the garage: fast to reach, but if the house floods, it's gone.
Cloud storage
Kept on a company's computers far away (iCloud, Google Drive, OneDrive). Like a storage unit: reachable from anywhere with the key.
Sync
Keeping both copies identical. Change it on your phone, it updates on your laptop.
"The cloud"
Just someone else's computer. A very well-guarded one.

Navigating the web

The internet and the web aren't the same thing, and knowing the difference makes everything else click.

Analogy

Roads, stores and your car

The internet is the road system connecting every town. The web is the stores and libraries along those roads. Your browser (Chrome, Safari, Edge, Firefox) is the car you drive to visit them.

A search engine like Google is the friendly gas-station clerk who knows where everything is. Email, video calls and apps also use the roads, they just aren't "stores."

Browser vocabulary

Tab
Another store you have open at the same time. Like bookmarks sticking out of a book.
Bookmark
A saved address so you can drive straight back.
Cookie
A coat-check ticket a site hands you so it remembers you next visit (your cart, your login).
Cache
Pieces of sites saved nearby so they load faster, like keeping groceries in the fridge.
Wi-Fi
Your home's invisible driveway onto the internet road.

Read a web address like a street address

Tap each part

Search smarter

  • Use quotes for an exact phrase: "sourdough starter"
  • Add site: to search one website: flu shot site:cdc.gov
  • Use a minus to leave something out: jaguar -car
  • Type like you're asking a librarian a question. Modern search handles full sentences well.
Watch out The first few results often say "Sponsored" or "Ad." Those are paid spots, not necessarily the best answer. For anything official (taxes, DMV, Medicare) make sure the address ends in .gov.
Why did the browser cookie go to the doctor? It was feeling crummy. So it cleared its cache and got some rest.

Apps & widgets

Apps do things. Widgets show things. Permissions decide what each one is allowed to touch.

Analogy

Appliances and fridge magnets

An app is an appliance. The blender blends, the toaster toasts; the weather app forecasts, the banking app banks. You open it, use it, close it.

A widget is a fridge magnet that shows info at a glance without opening anything: today's weather, your next appointment, your step count. It lives on your home screen and updates itself.

The app lifecycle

Install
Get it from the official store only: App Store (iPhone), Google Play (Android), Microsoft Store or the maker's real site.
Update
Say yes. Updates are mostly repairs and new locks, like a car recall notice.
Notifications
The app tapping your shoulder. You can turn off the chatty ones in Settings.
Uninstall
Hauling an old appliance to the curb. Frees up pantry space.

Permissions are house keys

When an app asks "Allow access to your location?" it's asking for a key to one room. Hand out keys that match the job.

Bots & AI assistants

A bot is software that does a task automatically. Some are simple. Some, like AI chat assistants, can hold a real conversation.

Analogy

Vending machine vs. well-read intern

A simple bot is a vending machine or a phone menu: "Press 1 for billing." It only knows the buttons it was built with.

An AI assistant (a chatbot you can talk to in plain English) is more like a very well-read intern. Quick, helpful, great at drafting and explaining. But interns can be confidently wrong, so you check the important stuff.

Bots you already meet

Website help chat "Your package shipped" texts Spam filters Voice assistants Automated phone menus AI writing helpers Social media spam accounts

Great uses for an AI assistant

  • "Explain this letter from my insurance in plain English."
  • "Help me write a polite email declining an invitation."
  • "Give me a 3-day meal plan with what's in my fridge."

How to talk to an AI assistant

  1. Give it the backstory. "I'm 67, new to iPhone, and want to share photos with my daughter" beats "how share photos."
  2. Say what you want back. A list? Three short steps? A friendly email?
  3. Ask follow-ups. "Simpler, please" or "What does that word mean?" is completely fine.
  4. Double-check anything that matters. Medical, legal, money and dates deserve a second source.
Never share Passwords, full Social Security numbers, bank account numbers or one-time codes. No real company bot needs your password in a chat window.
Why did the chatbot take a vacation? It needed to recharge. And it was tired of being asked to "just be human for a sec."

Security & encryption

Most online safety comes down to three habits: good locks (passwords), a second lock (two-step login), and not opening the door for strangers in fake uniforms (scams).

Analogy

Encryption is a lockbox in the mail

Sending a normal message online is like mailing a postcard. Anyone who handles it could read it. Encryption puts the message in a lockbox. The mail carrier can still deliver it, but only the person with the key can open it.

When a web address starts with https and shows a little padlock, your connection to that site is in a lockbox. Note: the lock means private, not trustworthy. Scam sites can have padlocks too.

Two-step login (2FA)

Like an ATM: you need the card (your password) and the PIN (a code texted to you or shown in an app). A thief with one isn't enough. Turn it on for email and banking first; email is the master key that resets everything else.

Spot a scam (phishing)

  • Urgency: "Your account closes in 24 hours!"
  • Odd sender: "amaz0n-support@mail-help.co"
  • Asks for codes, gift cards or remote access to your computer.
  • The fix: don't click. Open the company's app or type their address yourself.
I tried "incorrect" as my password. Now whenever I forget it, the computer reminds me: "Your password is incorrect."

When things go sideways

Most tech problems are solved by the same short checklist that professionals use. Work it top to bottom before you panic.

Analogy

Why restarting works

Remember the countertop? After hours of use it's cluttered with half-finished dishes, some of which the chef forgot about. Restarting wipes the counter clean and the manager sets up fresh. It fixes a surprising number of problems.

The calm-down checklist

  1. Is it plugged in, charged and switched on? Including the Wi-Fi router.
  2. Close the app and reopen it.
  3. Restart the device. A real restart, not just closing the lid.
  4. Check for updates.
  5. Search the exact error message in quotes, or paste it into an AI assistant.
  6. Ask someone, and show them a screenshot.
Wi-Fi fix Unplug the router, count to 30 slowly, plug it back in, and give it 2 full minutes. Yes, the 30 seconds matter: it lets the device fully power down.
What's the IT department's favorite song? "Have You Tried Turning It Off and On Again," the extended remix.
